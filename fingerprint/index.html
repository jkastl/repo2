Your Phone's Fingerprint Is Its Flaws

Every other exhibit on this site treats sensor error as something to cancel. This one turns it around. The accelerometer and gyroscope in your phone were each calibrated at the factory, and the leftover errors are small, stable, and unique to your phone.

That makes them an ID. Before the fixes described below, a web page could read them without asking, and the ID survived clearing cookies, private browsing, and a factory reset. This page shows the method on your own phone's sensor stream, entirely on your device.

Accelerations are in m/s² and include gravity, which is why a phone lying flat reads about 9.8 on one axis. Rotation rates are in degrees per second; a gyroscope at rest should read zero, and whatever it reads instead is its error. Real phones haven't been tested with this page yet, only simulated sensor data.

Offset and gain from gravity

Gravity is the one reference everyone carries. Lying flat, a perfect accelerometer reads exactly 1 g on one axis and zero on the other two. Turn it face down and that axis should read exactly −1 g. The average of the two readings is the axis's offset; half the difference, compared with 1 g, is its gain error. A gyroscope is even simpler: at rest it should read zero.

The gain check assumes standard gravity, 9.80665 m/s². Real gravity runs from about 9.78 m/s² at the equator to 9.83 at the poles, so any gain error smaller than about ±0.3% could be your latitude rather than your sensor. Offsets also drift as the phone warms up. Both are why the next step doesn't lean on gravity at all.

The grid

Inside the sensor, an analog-to-digital converter turns each reading into a whole number of counts. The phone then multiplies by a calibration gain measured for this unit at the factory, adds an offset, and hands the result to the browser. So every value the page sees is gain × whole number + offset. Zoom in far enough and the readings aren't continuous at all: they sit on a grid whose spacing is the factory's gain.

Finding the spacing is a greatest-common-divisor problem with decimals. Sort the distinct values, take the gaps between neighbours, and look for the largest step that every gap is a whole multiple of. Noise from the physical sensor doesn't blur it, because that noise happens before the counting: it moves a reading to a different grid point, never between two.

Your fingerprint

The grid steps are numbers the factory chose for this one phone, and they don't change: not when you clear cookies, not in a private window, not after a factory reset, because they live in the sensor's calibration, not in the browser. Round each step to 20 parts per million, hash the results, and you have an ID.

Press Keep this one, then capture again. The check compares the steps themselves, so it can tell the same phone from a different one even if a step happens to sit on a rounding boundary and flips the hex.

Bits, at most assumes each gain could fall anywhere in a ±1% band with every 20 ppm slice equally likely: log₂(2% ÷ 20 ppm) ≈ 10 bits per axis. Real gains aren't spread that evenly, and the axes of one chip may not vary independently, so treat it as a ceiling, not a measurement. The SensorID authors measured real iPhones instead: about 67 bits for the iPhone 6S, from its gyroscope and magnetometer.

The fix

The GCD trick needs every gap between readings to be an exact whole number of steps. So the cure is to break that exactness. Add a random amount of up to half a step to each count before the calibration is applied, and a reading can land anywhere in its cell instead of on a grid point. The gaps stop being whole multiples of anything, and the grid search comes back empty. The SensorID authors proposed exactly this kind of noise, and Apple added random noise in iOS 12.2. Apple's own security note says only that the issue "was addressed with improved motion sensor processing" (CVE-2019-8541).

Browsers took a blunter route. The W3C DeviceOrientation spec now requires readings to be limited to 0.1 m/s² and 0.1 °/s, and Chromium rounds them to those multiples, citing exactly this attack in its source. That puts every phone on the same grid, the browser's.

Neither costs ordinary motion features much. Walking swings the accelerometer by a few m/s² each step. On the simulated phone here, half a grid step is about 0.001 m/s², under a thousandth of that, and even rounding to 0.1 moves a reading by at most 0.05. How it will work

Capture: lay the phone flat and still for a few seconds, then flip it face down. The page records devicemotion samples. Gravity is a known 9.81 m/s², so any difference between what the sensor reports and 1 g is error.

The trick from the SensorID paper: the raw sensor outputs whole-number ADC counts, and the phone scales them by a per-device calibration gain before handing them to the browser. So the values the page sees sit on a grid whose spacing is that gain. Find the spacing (a greatest-common-divisor search over sample differences) and you have recovered the factory calibration without any special access.

The result: a short hex fingerprint built from the recovered gains, shown alongside how many bits of identity it might carry. Run it twice and it should match. Run it on a different phone and it shouldn't.

Privacy: nothing is sent anywhere. The page has no network calls, and the fingerprint is only displayed, never stored.

Build plan

  1. Sensor capture. Permission flow (DeviceMotionEvent.requestPermission() on iOS 13+, none needed on most Android browsers), a still-detector, and a raw-sample log view. On desktop, say plainly that there's no sensor.
  2. Offset and gain from gravity. Flat, then face down, then on an edge: fit per-axis bias and scale against ±1 g, and show the error vector next to a perfect sensor.
  3. Grid recovery. Estimate the ADC step per axis from the lattice the samples fall on: approximate GCD over pairwise differences, with a histogram that makes the lattice visible.
  4. Fingerprint and stability. Quantize the recovered values into a short ID, show it as a hex string and a small generated glyph, run a repeat-capture check, and give a rough entropy estimate.
  5. The defense. A toggle that adds the kind of noise Apple added in iOS 12.2, and the grid disappears. Explain why noise defeats the GCD trick while barely hurting motion features like step counting.
  6. Write-up. Tie it back to Ride Report and Scroll Report (the same sensor stream), fingerprinting in general, and the lesson: a stable error is information.

The deep cut

Zhang, Beresford, and Sheret presented SensorID at IEEE Security & Privacy in 2019. They could fingerprint iPhones from fewer than 100 motion samples, collected and processed in under a second, with an estimated 67 bits of entropy for the iPhone 6S. Apple fixed it in iOS 12.2 by adding random noise to the sensor output, and starting with iOS 13, Safari asks permission before sharing motion data at all.

The broader idea is older. Researchers had already shown that accelerometer imperfections could identify devices in 2014. Manufacturing tolerances that engineers calibrate away for accuracy are exactly what make each unit distinguishable.
