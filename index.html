Right Answers from Wrong Parts

In 1952 John von Neumann gave a set of lectures later published as Probabilistic Logics and the Synthesis of Reliable Organisms from Unreliable Components. Vacuum tubes failed all the time, and he wanted to know whether you could build a machine that was more trustworthy than anything inside it.

You can, and nearly everything you rely on does. Every part here is noisy, drifting, broken, or quietly lying, and each machine still gets the right answer. Each one also has a specific way it stops working. Each one is a live instrument on its own page, so go ahead and try to break it.

The common thread

All six machines make the same move. They add structured redundancy, meaning extra copies, extra bytes, an extra satellite, a second sensor, a second wavelength, or a deliberate drop, and they arrange it so the error becomes something you can measure and subtract.

All six also fail in a similar way: when the error stops behaving the way the design assumed. That can be copies that share a bug, noise that hits every byte instead of a few, satellites bunched together, a filter that trusts the wrong sensor, a calibration fitted on one population and used on everyone, or a buffer that hides the loss TCP needs to see. Redundancy only helps if the failures are independent and visible.
