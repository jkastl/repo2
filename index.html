A field manual in six instruments

Right Answers from Wrong Parts

In 1952 John von Neumann gave a set of lectures later published as Probabilistic Logics and the Synthesis of Reliable Organisms from Unreliable Components. Vacuum tubes failed all the time, and he wanted to know whether you could build a machine that was more trustworthy than anything inside it.

You can, and nearly everything you rely on does. Every part here is noisy, drifting, broken, or quietly lying, and each machine still gets the right answer. Each one also has a specific way it stops working. Every demo below is live, so go ahead and try to break it.

The Vote

triple modular redundancy · von Neumann, 1952–56

This is the oldest trick. Build three of the part, feed them the same question, and take the majority answer. If each part is right with probability p, the vote is right when at least two agree: 3p² − 2p³. Then you can do it again and vote on the votes.

Below, every green square is a component giving the right answer this instant and every red one is wrong. Circles are voters, and the lamp at the top is the only thing the outside world ever sees.

Try this

  1. Start at 0.80 reliability and add layers. The system error shrinks by roughly an order of magnitude each time, even though each part is still wrong one time in five.
  2. Drop reliability below 0.50. Voting now makes things worse, because the majority is wrong and gets more confident with every layer. The cobweb shows why: 0.5 is an unstable fixed point, and repeated voting pushes toward 0 or 1.
  3. Put reliability back up to 0.95, use 4 layers, and nudge common-mode faults to 0.02. Your 81-part system is now only as good as the thing all 81 share.

The deep cut

The math assumes failures are independent. On 4 June 1996, Ariane 5 flew with two inertial reference units for redundancy. Both ran the same software, so both hit the same 64-bit-to-16-bit overflow within milliseconds of each other and shut down. The rocket destroyed itself about 37 seconds after launch. The spare was a copy of the same mistake.

That's why serious redundancy uses dissimilar redundancy: different chips, different teams, different languages. Airbus flight-control computers pair units built on different processors with separately written software, so a single bug can't take out every copy.

Scratch the Code

Reed–Solomon over GF(256) · Reed & Solomon, 1960 · QR, Denso Wave, 1994

This is a real QR code, built from scratch on this page: version 3 at error-correction level H. Its 70 bytes are 26 bytes of message plus 44 bytes of Reed–Solomon parity, split into two interleaved blocks. Each block can locate and repair any 11 corrupted bytes without knowing in advance which ones they are.

Draw on it. Paint over modules, splat it, or slap a sticker on it. The decoder under the panel reads the damaged grid the same way a scanner would, finds the errors, and fixes them. You can also point your phone's camera at the screen and check that it still scans.

Try this

  1. Put a big sticker in the middle. That's how "logo in the middle" QR codes work: the logo is just damage the error correction absorbs.
  2. Hit clean, then sprinkle 30 flips a few times. Thirty scattered flipped modules can kill a code that shrugged off a sticker covering a hundred. Turn on the byte map to see why.
  3. Scribble a long line with the scribble brush while the map is on. Each byte is a small 2-wide snake of 8 modules, so a scratch across the grid only damages the bytes it passes through.

The deep cut

Reed–Solomon works on bytes, not bits. A byte with one flipped bit costs exactly as much to repair as a byte with all eight flipped. That's why it loves burst damage like scratches, stickers, and coffee rings, and why it's the outer code on CDs, DVDs, and the Voyager probes. Scattered single-bit noise is the case it handles worst, so CDs interleave their data twice to turn one scratch into many small bursts spread across different blocks.

The trick underneath is that a message becomes a polynomial, the parity bytes force that polynomial to have 22 known roots, and damage shows up as the polynomial no longer vanishing at those points. Twenty-two equations are enough to solve for 11 unknown positions and 11 unknown values. The format bits (the strips next to the big squares) carry their own separate BCH code, and they're stored twice.

Where Am I

GPS pseudoranging · side view, squashed to fit

Each GPS satellite carries an atomic clock and broadcasts the time. Your receiver notes when each broadcast arrives, multiplies the delay by the speed of light, and draws a circle. The catch is that your phone has a cheap quartz clock, and one microsecond of error is 300 m of range. None of these "ranges" are true. They're all wrong by the same unknown amount.

So GPS doesn't solve for where you are. It solves for where you are and what time it is, together, which is why you need one more satellite than you have dimensions. Slide the clock-error guess until the circles meet at a single point. That point is the fix.

  2. Look at the noise cloud. It's taller than it is wide, and the vertical DOP is worse than the horizontal. Every satellite is above you, so nothing pins you from below. That's why GPS altitude is always the sloppiest number.
  3. Drag all the satellites into a tight cluster. The cloud stretches into a long streak. Good satellites are spread out, not close.
  4. Switch off satellites until only two are left. Two circles and an unknown clock is three unknowns and two equations, so there's no unique answer.
  5. Press all sat clocks +10 µs. The fix doesn't move, and only the solved time changes. Then try one sat clock.

The deep cut

Satellite clocks tick faster than ground clocks by about 38 µs a day: weaker gravity up there speeds them up by about 45 µs, and orbital speed slows them by about 7 µs. They're built to run slow on the ground, at 10.22999999543 MHz instead of 10.23 MHz, so they're right in orbit.

You'll often read that without relativity "GPS would drift 10 km per day." The last button shows why that's only half right. An error that's the same on every satellite lands in the clock term you were already solving for, so position barely notices. What actually hurts is error that differs between satellites. Orbits are slightly eccentric, so each satellite's relativistic offset wobbles on its own schedule, and receivers correct for that term by term.

Two Wrong Answers

Kalman filter · Kalman, 1960 · Schmidt at NASA Ames, for Apollo

You have two ways to know where the car is, and both are wrong in different ways. GPS is noisy and slow (one jittery fix per second), but its errors don't add up. The accelerometer is fast and smooth, but you have to integrate it twice to get a position, so every tiny bias turns into error that grows with time squared.

A Kalman filter keeps a running guess and a running estimate of how wrong that guess is. The accelerometer moves the guess forward and inflates the uncertainty (the ellipse). Each GPS fix pulls the guess toward it, weighted by which of the two it currently trusts more.

Try this

  1. Watch the ellipse for a few seconds. It swells between GPS fixes and snaps smaller at each one, so you're watching the filter's confidence in real time.
  2. Hold the tunnel button for ten seconds. The filter coasts on the accelerometer, the ellipse balloons, and when GPS returns it snaps back without a jump.
  3. Push filter trusts GPS to 10× too much. The estimate twitches after every fix. Push it to 10× too little and it goes lazy and starts to drift. A badly tuned filter is overconfident about something.
  4. Watch the learned bias. The filter was never told the accelerometer is biased. It works that out from how its predictions keep missing the GPS fixes, and then corrects for it.

The deep cut

The last point is why this filter flew to the Moon. Its state isn't just position and velocity. It also includes the sensor's own error as something to estimate. Every phone, drone, and airliner running GPS-aided inertial navigation does the same thing: while GPS is available it calibrates the inertial sensors, so when GPS drops out the dead reckoning drifts far less.

Kalman's paper was met with skepticism. Stanley Schmidt at NASA Ames made it work with a linearized version for Apollo midcourse navigation, and it ran on a computer with about 4 KB of writable memory.

Light Through a Finger

pulse oximetry · Takuo Aoyagi, Nihon Kohden, 1972

A fingertip clip shines red (660 nm) and infrared (940 nm) light through skin, fat, bone, venous blood, nail polish, and arterial blood, all at once. It wants one thing out of that pile: what fraction of the hemoglobin in your arteries is carrying oxygen. Oxygenated blood is bright red and deoxygenated blood is dark, and at 940 nm the difference reverses.

Aoyagi's insight was to ignore everything that doesn't pulse. Only arterial blood swells with each heartbeat. Divide each color's pulsing part (AC) by its steady part (DC), then divide red by infrared. Skin, bone, and venous blood cancel out of that ratio of ratios, and what's left depends only on oxygen saturation.

Simulated finger. use my camera swaps in your own pulse: cover the rear camera (and flash, if your phone lets the page turn it on) with a fingertip and hold still. Video never leaves this page.

Try this

  1. Drop the true O₂ to 85 and watch the red trace grow relative to infrared. That growing red swing is the measurement.
  2. Turn on hand motion. Motion changes the light path for both colors by the same fraction, which pushes R toward 1, and here that reads as about 74%. Real oximeters show the same thing (their R = 1 sits near 82–85%), which is why a patient scratching their nose can set off a desaturation alarm.
  3. Make the finger cold (low perfusion). The pulsing signal shrinks toward the noise floor and the reading starts wandering, even though nothing about the blood changed.
  4. Press use my camera. A phone can get your heart rate, but not SpO₂: its camera sees broad color bands with no known wavelengths, and it has no calibration against real blood.

The deep cut

The ratio-of-ratios argument says skin pigment should cancel out, like everything else that doesn't pulse. In practice it doesn't fully cancel. In 2020, Sjoding and colleagues in the NEJM compared pulse-ox readings to arterial blood gases drawn at the same time. Among patients whose oximeter read a reassuring 92–96%, the blood was actually below 88% for 11.7% of Black patients versus 3.6% of White patients.

The calibration curve on the right isn't pure physics. Real light scatters instead of passing straight through, so every device maps R to SpO₂ with an empirical curve fitted on volunteers, and historically those volunteers were mostly light-skinned. You can't reproduce that bias with this simulator, and that's the lesson: the simple model assumes the effect away, just as the calibration did. It's the same dataset-shift problem as a model trained at one hospital and deployed at another. Carbon monoxide works similarly: CO-bound hemoglobin looks nearly the same as oxyhemoglobin at 660 nm, so a two-wavelength oximeter reports a poisoned patient as fine.

Drop It on Purpose

TCP congestion control · bufferbloat · CoDel (Nichols & Jacobson, 2012) · fq_codel (RFC 8290)

The internet delivers your bytes reliably over links that lose packets, and TCP depends on those losses. TCP has no idea how fast the path is, so it speeds up until a packet gets dropped, reads the drop as "too fast," backs off, and repeats. Van Jacobson added this in 1988 after the early internet went into congestion collapse.

Then memory got cheap. Routers and cable modems got huge buffers, so they could avoid dropping anything until a full second of traffic was queued. TCP never gets its signal, so it fills the buffer, and every packet behind it waits in line, including your video call. A router that refuses to drop packets is hiding the one signal TCP relies on.

Try this

  1. Leave it on plain FIFO with one upload. Ping starts near 30 ms, then climbs past a full second as TCP fills the buffer, and it stays there. Your link is 100% used and useless for anything interactive.
  2. Shrink the router buffer. Latency drops and throughput barely changes. The extra buffer wasn't adding speed, only delay.
  3. Switch to CoDel. It watches how long packets have been waiting, and once that stays above 5 ms for 100 ms it starts dropping, gently at first and then more often. TCP gets its signal early and the queue stays short.
  4. Switch to fq_codel and add uploads. Each flow gets its own queue, and sparse flows like your ping skip past the bulk. The "Smart Queues" checkbox on some home routers is this algorithm.

The deep cut

In October 1986 the link between Lawrence Berkeley Lab and UC Berkeley, 400 yards and a few router hops apart, dropped from 32 kbit/s to 40 bit/s. Every sender kept retransmitting into a network that was already drowning. Jacobson's fix, which treats loss as a congestion signal and backs off multiplicatively, is why the internet grew past that point.

Bufferbloat is that fix breaking from the opposite direction. Jim Gettys named it in 2010 while trying to work out why his home connection became unusable whenever he uploaded a file. The cure wasn't more bandwidth. It was to drop packets deliberately, before anyone has to wait.

The common thread

All six machines make the same move. They add structured redundancy, meaning extra copies, extra bytes, an extra satellite, a second sensor, a second wavelength, or a deliberate drop, and they arrange it so the error becomes something you can measure and subtract.

All six also fail in a similar way: when the error stops behaving the way the design assumed. That can be copies that share a bug, noise that hits every byte instead of a few, satellites bunched together, a filter that trusts the wrong sensor, a calibration fitted on one population and used on everyone, or a buffer that hides the loss TCP needs to see. Redundancy only helps if the failures are independent and visible.
