The Liar's Game

I'm thinking of a number from 1 to 1,000,000. Ask me yes-or-no questions. With honest answers, 20 questions always do it, because 2²⁰ is just over a million.

Now I'm allowed to lie once, and I won't tell you when. How many questions do you need? The answer is exactly 25, and the reason it's 25 is the same math that lets a QR code survive a sticker.

How it will work

You ask, the page lies. The page never picks a number. It's an adversary: after every question it chooses whichever answer keeps the most possibilities alive, lying whenever a lie hurts you more than the truth. You can't out-guess it by luck, only by asking well.

The board shows every candidate number as a cell colored by how many lies it would take for that number to be the answer: 0 lies (still clean), 1 lie (suspect), or more than the allowed lies (dead). Asking a question splits the board, and whichever side the page says "no" to gets one lie closer to dead.

The volume meter is the heart of it. Berlekamp's trick: give every candidate a weight equal to the number of answer sequences you still can't rule out for it. The total weight can at most halve with each question. If the weight exceeds 2q with q questions left, you're provably stuck. The meter shows that bound live, so you can watch a bad question waste your budget.

Reverse mode: you pick the number and you lie. The page asks the questions and still finds you, in the minimum number the math allows.

Build plan

  1. Core math, no UI. A state is a vector of counts: how many candidates have used 0, 1, …, k lies.
    • Berlekamp volume: Vq(state) = Σi ai · Σj≤k−i C(q, j).
    • The adversary answers to maximize the volume that remains, breaking ties toward keeping more clean candidates.
    • Test: a state is winnable in q questions only if Vq ≤ 2q. Check against the known 1-lie result (10⁶ needs 25).
  2. Small board, 1 lie. Numbers 1–64, one allowed lie, question = "is it in this set?" built by tapping cells or using presets (≥ n, bit k is 1, even).
    • Cells colored by lies used, a question history, and the volume meter against 2q.
  3. Reverse mode. The page asks using a volume-halving strategy. You answer with Yes/No and may lie up to k times, and it announces your number.
  4. Scale and settings. 1–1,000,000 with 0–3 lies (the board becomes a histogram of the lie-count classes), and a "kid mode" with 1–20 and picture questions.
  5. Write-up. The link to codes: each question is a bit, lies are bit errors, and the questioner is designing an error-correcting code one bit at a time. Add the "try this" list and the deep cut.

The deep cut

Stanisław Ulam posed the question in his 1976 autobiography, Adventures of a Mathematician, and Alfréd Rényi had described a version in 1961. Andrzej Pelc settled the one-lie case in 1987: for a million numbers it's 25 questions. Each extra lie costs more, and the exact answers for larger k took years of casework.

What makes it more than a puzzle is that it's error-correcting codes with feedback. Because you hear each answer before asking the next question, you can adapt, and adaptive questioning sometimes needs fewer questions than any fixed code.
