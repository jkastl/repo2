Tear-Proof Secrets

Type the Wi-Fi password, pick "5 tags, any 3", and print five small QR tags. Stick them on the fridge, in a drawer, in the car. Lose any two and scan the remaining three to get the password back.

It's the same polynomial math as Scratch the Code, applied to objects instead of bytes. Pick the secret mode and something stranger happens: any two tags together reveal nothing at all about the password.

How it will work

Two modes, one polynomial idea. Any k points determine a polynomial of degree k − 1. Hand out n points on it, and any k of them rebuild it.

  • Backup mode (Reed–Solomon erasure code). The data are the polynomial's coefficients. It's compact, since each tag holds about 1/k of the data, but each tag reveals part of the secret.
  • Secret mode (Shamir, 1979). The secret is only the constant term, and the other coefficients are random. With fewer than k tags, every possible secret is equally likely. Each tag is as big as the secret.

Split: enter text, choose n and k, and get n printable tags. Each tag is a QR code plus a short human-typeable code and a label like "tag 2 of 5 · any 3".

Recover: scan tags with the camera (or type them in), and the page shows the polynomial being pinned down one point at a time, then reveals the secret once it has k.

Build plan

  1. Math core. GF(256) arithmetic (reuse from the main page's QR code), Lagrange interpolation, and share and recover for both modes, byte by byte.
    • Round-trip tests for every k-subset of n ≤ 8. In secret mode, check that the k − 1 share distribution doesn't depend on the secret.
  2. Visual explainer. An interactive plot over the real numbers (for intuition): drag points, watch the unique curve through k of them, and see that k − 1 points fit infinitely many curves through every possible secret.
  3. Split and print. Text in, n/k chooser, tags out: QR (reuse the encoder, generalized beyond version 3), a base32 text form with a checksum, and a print stylesheet that lays tags out for cutting.
  4. Recover. Paste or type tag codes, with duplicate and wrong-set detection (tags carry a short set ID). Then camera scanning via BarcodeDetector where the browser supports it, and typed codes elsewhere.
  5. Write-up. Backup vs secret mode tradeoffs, why this is the basis for key backup in hardware wallets (SLIP-39) and for erasure coding in storage systems, and what it doesn't protect against (a thief who finds k tags).

The deep cut

Adi Shamir's 1979 paper, “How to share a secret,” is two pages long. Its security isn't "hard to break"; it's information-theoretic. With k − 1 shares, every secret is exactly as likely as every other, so there's nothing to compute, even with infinite computing power.

The erasure-code version is why a storage cluster can lose whole drives without losing files. It's also why a Reed–Solomon decoder repairs twice as much damage when it's told which bytes are missing (erasures) as when it has to find them: a known-missing byte costs half as much parity as an unknown wrong one.
