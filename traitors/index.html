Lab · 08 blueprint

Traitors, Pass-the-Phone

A party game for 3–7 players and one phone. You're generals around a besieged city. The commander sends an order, attack or retreat, and the loyal generals win only if they all act on the same order. The phone secretly makes some of you traitors, and traitors lie to whoever they like.

Play it with three people and one traitor and the loyal side can't win reliably, no matter how clever they are. Add a fourth player and they can. That isn't a balance problem; it's a theorem from 1982, and the game makes you find it by losing.

Instrument not built yetSetup, secret roles, and the message rounds go here. See the build plan below.

How it will work

Setup: player names (like Scatter), then how many traitors. The phone deals roles in secret: each player takes the phone, taps to reveal their role, and passes it on. One player is the commander, who may also be a traitor.

Round 1: the commander privately sends each lieutenant an order. The phone shows only the recipient's own inbox, so a traitor commander can tell different people different things.

Round 2 (the relay): each lieutenant tells every other lieutenant, in private, what the commander told them. Traitors may relay anything. This is Lamport's Oral Messages algorithm, OM(1).

Decide: each loyal lieutenant takes the majority of everything they heard. The phone then reveals the full message graph: who said what to whom, with every lie in red, and whether the loyal generals agreed.

The twist: after a few games, the phone offers signed orders, where a forged relay is detectable. With signatures, agreement works with any number of traitors. That's the difference between plain voting and what blockchains and flight computers actually do.

Build plan

  1. Protocol engine, no UI. OM(m) as a pure function: inputs are roles, the commander's orders, and each traitor's relay choices; outputs are every loyal decision and whether they agree.
    • Unit-check the classic results: n = 3, m = 1 can fail; n = 4, m = 1 always succeeds; n = 7, m = 2 always succeeds.
  2. Hot-seat flow for OM(1). Setup screen, secret role reveal with a "pass to NAME" screen between every private step so nobody sees someone else's inbox, commander round, relay round, decisions.
    • Traitors choose what to send with two big buttons. Loyal players just confirm the relay.
  3. The reveal. An animated message graph with players around a circle, arrows for every message, lies in red, and a verdict. A tally across games of "loyal wins at n = 3 vs n = 4".
  4. Bot players. Fill empty seats with bots (honest or traitor) so two people can play a 4-player game, and a solo "watch it run" mode that loops random games at 3 vs 4 players.
  5. Signed messages, SM(m). Orders carry a seal that traitors can't forge, only withhold. Show agreement surviving 1 traitor out of 3.
  6. Write-up. Why 3f + 1 (the indistinguishability argument drawn as two scenarios the loyal lieutenant can't tell apart), fly-by-wire and the Boeing 777's triple-triple architecture, and why Bitcoin swapped identity for proof-of-work.

The deep cut

The problem came out of NASA-funded work at SRI on SIFT, a fault-tolerant flight-control computer, in the late 1970s. Engineers had assumed a failed processor just stops or sends garbage. Pease, Shostak, and Lamport showed that a component that fails by sending different wrong values to different peers needs more than 3× redundancy for every fault you want to survive. They called it "Byzantine" so no living nationality would be offended.

The 3-player impossibility is worth feeling from the inside. A loyal lieutenant who hears "attack" from the commander and "the commander told me retreat" from the other lieutenant has no way to know which of the two is lying. Both stories fit what they saw.
